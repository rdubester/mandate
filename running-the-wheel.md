# Running the Wheel
### Off-archetype pivots in unbalanced matchups

**The core idea.** Every faction's shard includes tools from its two adjacent colours. So a
faction that is losing a matchup does not have to keep fighting on the axis where it loses —
it can *run around the wheel* into colours its opponent counters less, borrow those tools, and
bend them toward its own win condition. The favourable position keeps moving, so matchups stay
dynamic instead of settling.

---

### Two conditions

The factions are tuned for one ideal: a balanced 5-player game where everyone full-sends their
dominant (centre) archetype should be roughly fair. But most real situations are *not* that —
a 1v1, or any partial table, is unbalanced by default. Running the wheel is the in-game answer
to "my opponent counters me." It is what makes the unbalanced situations playable.

### What a pivot is — and isn't

A pivot means shifting weight onto your **wing** colours — the two non-centre actions in your
shard — *not* switching win conditions. White leaning blue builds a card-draw engine; it does
not try to win *as* blue. The borrowed tools feed white's actual goal (landing decrees for the
Mandate). You change which tools you lean on; your win condition never moves.

### Worked example: Red vs. White

In the first red/white prototype, red beats white naively. Red's hard removal is overwhelming,
and white on decrees alone cannot build a durable advantage — it does not compound from its
cards fast enough to outrun red. White's escape is to run the wheel:

- **Lean blue.** White's early problem against red is too little card draw. White uses blue
  tools to build a card-draw engine that red has a harder time disrupting, then rides that
  velocity to draw and land decrees.
- **Lean green.** Red counters white hard but counters green far less. White goes unit-heavy,
  amasses popular support, and channels that mass into pushing its Mandate through.

### The chase

Pivots provoke counter-pivots, and no position stays stable:

1. White is losing to red → White leans **green** (red counters green weakly).
2. Red answers by leaning **black** (black counters green, and black is in red's shard).
3. White leans **blue** (black does not counter blue, and blue is in white's shard).
4. Red falls back on its **core removal** (red counters blue natively) → White is pushed back
   toward green, and the loop repeats.

It is not a clean rock-paper-scissors triangle — it is rock-paper-scissors *flavoured*. Each
pivot moves you onto ground your opponent currently counters less, which forces them to
re-commit, which opens a fresh gap. The skill is reading where the opponent has committed and
pivoting into the opening.

### Why the wheel makes this work (the red/white case)

Red sits diagonal to both white and blue, so red counters both. What red does *not* counter
well is green and black — its allies. White holds blue and green; red holds black and green;
and blue and black do not counter each other. So each side always has live counter-tools
available — they simply have to lean into them.

### Thematic payoff

The pivots mean something. White is the republic — rule of law — and it cannot out-fight red,
the military. So white turns to the people: it goes green, builds mass popular support, and
rides that to install its Mandate. The mechanical pivot reads as a story.

### Design implication

You cannot build only red's cards and only white's cards, sit them across from each other, and
expect an interesting game. The cross-shard wing tools are not flavour or filler — they *are*
the strategic depth. A naive mono-vs-mono matchup being unbalanced (red > white) is not
necessarily a bug to patch by flattening the factions; it is the problem that running the
wheel exists to solve. Build the wing tools in, and the imbalance becomes a dynamic rather
than a defect.
